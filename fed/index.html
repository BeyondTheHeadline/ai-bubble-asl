What does a Fed rate hike actually mean? Explained in ASL — full English transcript

Short answer: When the Fed “raises rates,” it raises the target range for one very short-term rate — the federal funds rate banks charge each other for overnight loans (from 3.50%–3.75% to 3.75%–4% on September 16, 2026). That rate sits at the bottom of a “ladder” of rates and pushes up Treasury bills, business loans, car loans, credit cards, and mortgages over time, which slows demand. It cannot fix supply-driven inflation, and its full effect on prices and jobs takes months to show up.

FAQ

What does it mean when the Fed raises rates?
It does not mean the Fed orders every bank to raise every loan. On September 16, 2026, the Fed raised the target range for the federal funds rate by 0.25%, from 3.50%–3.75% to 3.75%–4%, saying inflation was still too high. Watch this part
What is the federal funds rate?
It is the interest rate on very short-term loans between banks. Banks keep reserves in accounts at the Federal Reserve; a bank that ends the day short can borrow reserves from another bank, typically overnight, and the rate on that loan is the federal funds rate. Watch this part
How does the Fed keep rates in its target range?
Through Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB), the interest the Fed pays banks on reserves, raised to 3.90% in September. A bank that can earn about 3.9% at the Fed with less risk will not lend overnight for 3%, so borrowers must pay more — and that pressure climbs a ladder of rates to Treasury bills, business loans, car financing, credit cards, bonds, and mortgages. Watch this part
What is inflation?
Inflation means prices across the economy in general are rising over time. If inflation falls from 6% to 3%, prices did not get cheaper — they are just rising more slowly. It can come from demand that is too strong, from supply problems like war, bad weather, tariffs, or housing shortages, or from both. Watch this part
Can raising interest rates fix all inflation?
No. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, so people and businesses buy less and demand decreases. But the Fed cannot create oil, produce food, or build houses, so it cannot directly fix inflation caused by supply problems. Watch this part
How does a Fed rate hike affect me?
It depends who you are. A new mortgage, a car loan, or credit-card debt gets more expensive. An existing fixed mortgage stays the same, and savers may benefit because savings, money-market, and short-term investment rates may pay more. Watch this part
Why is the Fed decision so hard?
Congress gave the Fed two goals — price stability and maximum employment — and they can conflict: higher rates can bring inflation down but also slow hiring and cost jobs. The effects also arrive with a time lag, so the Fed must decide now without seeing the full impact of past decisions. Watch this part
