What does a Fed rate hike actually mean? Explained in ASL — full English transcript

Short answer: On September 16, 2026, the Federal Reserve raised its target interest rate by 0.25%—from a range of 3.50%–3.75% to 3.75%–4%—because inflation was still too high. That target is the federal funds rate, a very short-term overnight rate between banks. It can influence car loans, credit cards, mortgages, and even hiring, but it cannot create oil, produce food, or build houses.

FAQ

What did the Fed do on September 16, 2026?
The Federal Reserve announced it was raising its target interest rate by 0.25% because inflation was still too high. The target range moved from 3.50%–3.75% to 3.75%–4%. That 0.25% sounds small, but the video explains it can affect the cost of a car, credit-card debt, business borrowing, buying a home, and even hiring. Watch this part
What is the federal funds rate?
It is a very short-term interest rate inside the banking system: the rate on overnight loans of reserves between banks. When people say the Fed raised rates, they mean the Fed raised the target range for that rate—not that it ordered every bank to raise every loan by the same amount. Watch this part
If inflation falls from 6% to 3%, did prices get cheaper?
No. Inflation means prices across the economy are rising over time. If inflation goes from 6% to 3%, prices are still increasing—just more slowly. Lower inflation is not the same thing as lower prices. Watch this part
How does a Fed rate hike reach mortgages, car loans, and credit cards?
Banks keep reserves in accounts at the Fed, and the Fed pays Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB)—raised to 3.90% in September—to keep the federal funds rate near its target. That short-term rate sits at the bottom of a ladder that can influence Treasury bills and money-market rates, then business loans, adjustable-rate loans, some credit-card rates, and car financing, and farther up, business bonds, long-term government bonds, and home mortgages. Watch this part
Can the Fed fix every kind of inflation by raising rates?
No. Rate hikes work on demand: borrowing gets more expensive, people and businesses buy less, and total spending can fall. If inflation comes from a supply problem—a war that cuts oil, bad weather that destroys crops, or not enough houses—the Fed cannot create oil, produce food, or build houses. It can only reduce demand. Watch this part
Does a Fed rate increase affect everyone the same way?
No. If you need a new mortgage, a new car, or carry a lot of credit-card debt, borrowing can get more expensive. If you already have a house, your existing mortgage stays the same. If you have savings, savings rates, money-market rates, and short-term investments may pay more. Watch this part
What two goals has Congress given the Fed?
Price stability and maximum employment. The Fed wants inflation under control and a strong job market at the same time. Those goals can conflict: raising rates to slow demand can help inflation come down, but it can also cause businesses to invest less, hire less, and—if the economy slows too much—cost people their jobs. The full impact also takes time; financial markets can react quickly, while the real economy adjusts over weeks and months. Watch this part

If the average price of that overall group continues increasing over time, that's inflation. And there's something really important to understand here. Suppose inflation is 6%.

Banks, reserves, and the Fed [5:36]

Banks can borrow reserves from one another through very short-term loans. Typically, a bank borrows the money overnight and then pays it back. The interest rate on that type of loan between banks is the federal funds rate.

That is the rate for which the Fed just announced its target.

Interest on reserves and the rate ladder [6:58]

So how does the Fed get banks to stay around that target range of 3.75% to 4%? Through another type of interest rate. Banks keep their reserves at the Fed, right?

The Fed pays them interest for keeping money there. That interest is called Interest on Reserve Balances, or IORB. Recently, in September, the Fed raised that rate to 3.90%.

Why is that important? Imagine you're a bank. You have $100 million sitting there at the Fed.

You can leave it there and earn around 3.9%. Another bank comes to you and says: "Hey, can I borrow that money overnight? I'll pay you 3% interest." You're not going to do it.

Why? Because you can earn more by leaving your money in reserves at the Fed, and there's less risk. That means if another bank or financial institution wants to borrow that money, it has to be willing to pay a higher interest rate.

That's how the interest rate set by the Fed can influence other interest rates and begin affecting the entire economy. Remember the ladder with the different levels of interest rates? At the bottom is the federal funds rate.

That's the very short-term rate on overnight loans between banks. That can influence rates above it: Treasury bills. Money-market rates.
