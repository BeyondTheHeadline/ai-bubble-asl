America is $40 trillion in debt — what happens when the bill comes due? ASL explainer with full English transcript

Short answer: U.S. government debt is now more than $40 trillion because Congress keeps spending more than it collects (about $7.4 trillion spent vs. $5.6 trillion collected in 2026, a deficit of almost $1.9 trillion) and borrows the gap by selling Treasuries. Interest alone is now about $1 trillion a year. A crisis is not happening tomorrow, but debt and interest cannot grow faster than the economy forever: the ways out are growth, higher taxes, spending cuts — or “pay later.”

FAQ

How much is the U.S. national debt?
U.S. government debt is now more than $40 trillion. In 1863, during the Civil War, it reached $1 billion. Watch this part
Why does the U.S. government keep borrowing?
Congress sets taxes and spending. In 2026 the government expects to collect around $5.6 trillion and spend around $7.4 trillion, a deficit of almost $1.9 trillion. It covers the gap by selling Treasury bills, notes, and bonds, so each new deficit adds to the debt. Watch this part
How much interest does the U.S. pay on its debt?
About $1 trillion a year just for interest. Higher interest costs make the deficit harder to shrink, which means more borrowing: deficit → borrow → debt increases → interest costs increase. Watch this part
Why does the world keep lending to America?
Trust. The U.S. economy is huge, the government can collect taxes, the dollar is used around the world, and the Treasury market is enormous, so U.S. government debt is considered one of the safest investments in the world. Watch this part
Why can't the Fed just print money to pay the debt?
Creating another $1 trillion does not create more houses, food, energy, doctors, or goods — just more dollars. If the economy doesn't grow enough to keep up, more money competes for the same stuff, which pushes prices and inflation up. Watch this part
How big is the debt compared with the economy?
The U.S. economy produces around $32 trillion of goods and services a year (GDP), while the debt is more than $40 trillion — around 125% of GDP, projected to reach about 137% by 2036. What matters most is whether the economy or the debt grows faster. Watch this part
How can the U.S. get out of debt?
The best option is a booming economy, but growth cannot be ordered. The government can directly collect more (raise taxes) or spend less (cut programs), but both cost votes, so politicians tend to borrow more and pay later. Watch this part
What happens if investors stop trusting U.S. debt?
Investors may demand more interest, which raises borrowing costs, enlarges the deficit, and requires even more borrowing — a loop. Combined with a recession, the worst case means job losses, falling retirement accounts, rising prices and taxes, and cut services. The video says this is not happening tomorrow, but “math is math.” Watch this part

Full English transcript

English captions for America is $40 Trillion in Debt. What Happens When the Bill Comes Due? (Beyond the Headline / @BeyondTheHeadLineASL). Timestamps match the published video chapters.

Intro [0:00]

$40 trillion: how did we get here? [0:15]

U.S. government debt is now more than $40 trillion. That number is so large, it’s hard to even visualize. How long did it take us to get there? In 1863, during the Civil War, U.S. debt reached $1 billion. Fast-forward to 1981: debt reached $1 trillion.

Fast-forward just 45 years. Now: more than $40 trillion. How did that happen? Where does all that debt come from? Why is it increasing so fast? Can it just keep increasing? And if it does... What happens? That’s what we’re going to analyze today.

Taxes, spending & the deficit [1:02]

Let’s start with Congress. Congress passes laws that determine how the government collects taxes: Income taxes. Payroll taxes. Corporate taxes. And others. Congress also passes laws determining how the government spends money:

Social Security. Medicare. Medicaid. Defense. Infrastructure. Other government programs. This year, 2026, the government expects to collect around: $5.6 trillion. And spend around: $7.4 trillion. That leaves a gap of almost: $1.9 trillion. That gap is called the deficit.

The government is spending more than it collects.

How the government borrows [1:52]

So how does it pay for that gap? It borrows. How? The U.S. Treasury sells debt: Treasury bills. Treasury notes. Treasury bonds. Who buys them? People. Banks. Retirement funds. Investment funds. Foreign governments. Other investors. Suppose you give the government $1,000.

The government gives you a Treasury security. It promises to pay you back later, plus interest. Now look at the chain: Congress passes tax and spending laws. The government spends more than it collects. That creates a deficit. The deficit means the government has to borrow.

And borrowing causes the debt to increase. Next year there’s another deficit? Borrow again. Debt increases again. The next year? Again. Again. Again.

$1 trillion a year in interest [2:55]

But remember: When the Treasury borrows money, it promises to... PAY IT BACK — PLUS INTEREST. So when the debt reaches $40 trillion... How much does it cost just to pay the interest on all that debt? About $1 trillion a year just for interest on the debt. Think about that.

The government has to pay that interest while also paying for: Social Security... Medicare... Medicaid... Defense... Roads... Schools... The government already has an enormous bill just from the interest on money it previously borrowed. And that’s where the problem starts feeding itself. The government already has a deficit.

DEFICIT → BORROW → DEBT INCREASES → INTEREST COSTS INCREASE Higher interest costs make the deficit even harder to shrink. Then what? Borrow again. So the question becomes:

Why the world keeps lending: trust [3:55]

Why do people, businesses, and countries keep lending America all this money? The main reason: TRUST. Investors around the world have historically trusted the U.S. government to repay its debt. The U.S. economy is huge. The government has the power to collect taxes.

The U.S. dollar is used around the world. The Treasury market is enormous. U.S. government debt is considered one of the safest investments in the world. So people, banks, retirement funds, governments... keep buying it. That trust allows the U.S. government to borrow enormous amounts of money.

Can the Fed just print money? [4:41]

Who else can buy Treasuries? The Federal Reserve. How does the Fed pay for them? It can create new money electronically and add it to the banking system. That’s what people mean when they say: “PRINTING MONEY.” But there’s an important difference: When the Treasury sells securities to borrow money... GOVERNMENT DEBT INCREASES.

When the Fed creates new money to buy securities... NEW MONEY ENTERS THE FINANCIAL SYSTEM. But both can happen at the same time. So now the question is: If the Fed can create money... Why not just keep printing whatever we need forever? Imagine tomorrow the Fed creates another: $1 trillion.

Does that suddenly mean America has more houses? More food? More energy? More doctors? More medicine? More factories? More goods and services? No. We simply have: MORE DOLLARS. If the economy is growing quickly and producing more, it may be able to keep up with that additional money. But if it doesn’t...

More money is competing to buy the same amount of stuff. That puts upward pressure on prices. Inflation rises.

Debt vs. the economy (GDP) [6:05]

Now bring this back to the debt. We know the debt is: $40 trillion. But $40 trillion compared with what? We need to know how large the U.S. economy is. Why? A larger economy means more income, more business activity, more tax revenue...

and a greater ability to support that debt. So we need to compare: DEBT vs. ECONOMY. Today, the U.S. economy produces around: $32 trillion worth of goods and services each year. That’s called: GDP. U.S. debt: More than $40 trillion.

That means the value of everything the economy produces in an entire year is still less than the debt. Is that automatically terrible? It depends. First, we need to ask: Which one is growing faster? If the economy grows faster than the debt? Good. The debt becomes easier to manage.

But if the debt grows faster than the economy? The burden becomes greater and greater.

Where the debt is headed [7:06]

Current projections show the debt continuing to rise relative to the economy. Right now, debt is around 125% of GDP. By 2036, it’s projected to rise to around 137%. Interest costs are also projected to keep increasing. So if this continues...

Something eventually has to change, right? What are our options?

Way out #1: the economy booms [7:31]

The best option? The economy BOOMS. If the economy grows extremely fast: More jobs. More businesses. More income. More tax revenue. The debt becomes smaller relative to the economy. Great. But the government can’t simply order the economy: “Boom now!” Growth takes time. It requires things like: New technology. New investment.

New businesses. Higher productivity. Maybe AI creates a huge productivity boom. Maybe. But we can’t guarantee that.

Way out #2: collect more or spend less [8:06]

So what can the government directly change? Two big things: COLLECT MORE. SPEND LESS. Collect more means: RAISE TAXES. Who pays? Workers? Businesses? Higher-income people? Spend less means what? Cut Social Security? Medicare? Medicaid? Defense?

Other government programs? That means somebody receives: LESS. Here’s the problem. People want benefits. They don’t want those benefits cut. And they also don’t want: HIGHER TAXES.

Why politicians choose "pay later" [8:41]

And politicians need what? VOTES. They need people to elect them. So what choices do politicians face? Cut benefits? People get angry. Raise taxes? People get angry. Either choice can cost them votes... and maybe their jobs. So what’s the politically easier choice? Force people to sacrifice today? No. Instead: Borrow more. Debt increases. PAY LATER.

New election. New Congress. Same problem. Again. Again. Again. That’s why this problem is so difficult to fix. Fixing it now causes pain now. Borrowing allows us to postpone that pain until later. So we keep deciding: PAY LATER. But what happens when “later” finally arrives?

When trust slips [9:33]

Remember earlier when we talked about why people, businesses, and countries keep lending America money? TRUST. Now suppose the debt keeps growing faster than the economy. Interest expenses continue increasing. Eventually, investors may start getting concerned.

America wants to borrow another trillion dollars? Okay. But now investors say: I WANT MORE INTEREST. That makes government borrowing more expensive. Interest costs increase. That makes the deficit larger. The government needs to borrow even more. Investors become even more concerned. So they demand even higher interest rates.

Loop. Loop. Loop.

Recession, printing & the worst case [10:23]

Now suppose a recession hits. Stock and bond markets can fall. Retirement accounts lose value. And the recession feeds directly back into the government’s problem. People earn less. Businesses earn less. So the government collects less in taxes. But the government still has to pay: Social Security. Medicare. Defense.

Government programs. And now it also has an enormous: DEBT INTEREST PAYMENT. Remember those two choices politicians avoided earlier by choosing to borrow instead? Now those choices become harder to avoid. Cut spending. Raise taxes. Borrow even more. Or print more money. Printing more money can push prices and inflation higher.

Food. Homes. Electricity. Medicine. Everything families need becomes more expensive. Your savings buy less. Your paycheck buys less. Now imagine the worst case: Several of these things happen at the same time. You lose your job. Your retirement account falls. Loans become more expensive.

Prices are rising. Taxes are rising. Government benefits or services are being cut. People lose their homes. Businesses close. Unemployment rises. Families become poorer. Anger grows. Political instability grows. And the government has less ability to help.

Is this happening tomorrow? [11:59]

That’s the worst-case scenario. Am I saying: THIS HAPPENS TOMORROW? No. The point is that a government debt problem can eventually become: YOUR MONEY PROBLEM. YOUR JOB PROBLEM. YOUR RETIREMENT PROBLEM. YOUR FAMILY PROBLEM. Again: Will all of that happen tomorrow? NO. But math is math. Debt and interest cannot grow faster than the economy forever without something

eventually changing.

What could change the outlook [12:29]

Maybe the economy booms. Maybe AI helps create a huge increase in economic growth. Maybe Congress finally makes those two difficult choices: Taxes and spending. But if every year we choose: PAY LATER... PAY LATER... Does the bill disappear? No. We can debate when the bill comes due. We can debate how we’ll pay it. But pretending it isn’t there? That’s foolish.

Someone will eventually have to pay. You. Me. Our children. Thank you for watching. Let me know in the comments if you agree, disagree, or think there’s something we need to discuss more. Thanks. Peace.
